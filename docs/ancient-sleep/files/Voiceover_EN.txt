Ancient people faced versions of these questions in very different places. Archaeologists have even found traces of the beds they prepared.

To understand those choices, let's give our little traveller a simple task: find somewhere to sleep before dark.

This is an imaginary campsite, but the trade-offs are easy to understand. A roof does not make a sharp stone comfortable. A soft surface does not stop the rain. And a place that looks pleasant in daylight can feel very different after dark.

A cave is one possibility. People also built shelters.

At Ohalo Two, beside the Sea of Galilee, archaeologists uncovered the remains of a camp about twenty-three thousand years old. Its inhabitants fished, hunted, gathered plants, and lived in huts made from vegetation.

Inside one hut, researchers found deliberately arranged grass bedding on the floor. They reconstructed a space with a hearth, working areas, and a likely sleeping area near the walls.

Picture the hut from above. Different activities occupy different parts of the same small space. Somewhere to work. Somewhere for the fire. Somewhere to settle down.

There are no separate bedrooms in that reconstruction, but there is organization.

For our traveller, this adds a useful possibility: a sleeping place can be made. You can modify the space around you instead of waiting for the landscape to provide the perfect room.

The hut also leaves us with a more interesting question. Once you have a place to lie down, how do you make the floor worth lying on?

For that, jump much farther back, to Border Cave in southern Africa.

Researchers identified traces of grass bedding dating to around two hundred thousand years ago. People had brought bundles of grass into the cave and spread them over layers of ash.

A sleeping surface, assembled from available materials.

The ash is especially interesting. The researchers proposed that it helped create a cleaner base and discourage crawling insects. Some came from older bedding that had been burned, suggesting that these surfaces were maintained and renewed.

The grass-covered areas also served as places to work. A bed could be part of a shared living surface, used differently through the day.

We cannot ask its makers how comfortable it felt. But the remains show deliberate preparation.

Unfortunately, comfort can attract complaints from creatures much smaller than you.

At Sibudu, a rock shelter in South Africa, bedding from about seventy-seven thousand years ago included stems and leaves of grass-like plants called sedges. On top were leaves from a tree known as river wild-quince.

Those leaves contain chemicals with insecticidal properties. Researchers interpret their presence as evidence that people may have selected plants to help control pests.

The choice of leaf could matter alongside the choice of location.

Later layers at the same site show bedding being burned, interpreted as a way of maintaining the occupied space.

That does not reveal a universal cleaning schedule. It reveals people repeatedly managing the places where they lived.

Our traveller inspects a leaf with new respect.

Finding somewhere to sleep was only the beginning of keeping it usable.

There is another part of a sleeping place that will never appear in a pile of grass: the people around it.

To explore that, we need to leave prehistory and look at a modern study.

Researchers measured night-time activity among Hadza hunter-gatherers in Tanzania. Across twenty days, the monitored participants were all classified as asleep at the same time for just eighteen minutes in total.

People fell asleep, woke up, and stirred at different times. Their sleep schedules also varied with age.

The researchers suggested that this overlap could provide opportunities for someone to notice a disturbance during the night.

These measurements came from movement monitors. They do not establish that somebody was consciously standing guard every minute.

And the Hadza are a present-day community with their own history. Their sleep cannot simply be copied into an ancient camp.

The study does illustrate a possibility worth considering: sleeping among other people can create a very different situation from sleeping alone.

Our traveller quietly moves his bedding a little closer to the group.

Now the sun is setting. Does everybody immediately fall asleep?

Modern research gives us a reason to question that familiar picture.

A study of three communities living without industrial lifestyles found that participants generally began sleeping several hours after sunset. Their sleep also varied with the season.

That gives us examples of life without a modern bedroom, while leaving the exact bedtime of a person at Border Cave unknown.

Archaeology preserves fragments: a floor, a hearth, a layer of plants. From those fragments, researchers build explanations with different levels of certainty.

They can identify carefully arranged bedding without knowing who occupied each patch. They can reconstruct a likely sleeping area without recovering a single dream.

Our cartoon traveller has taken us between places separated by enormous distances and stretches of time. The real people in those places had their own surroundings, materials, habits, and problems to solve.

So how did ancient people choose where to sleep?

There was no single answer across all of prehistory. The surviving evidence shows people using natural shelter, building living spaces, preparing plant bedding, and maintaining the surfaces they occupied.

Choosing somewhere to sleep could involve several connected decisions: the place, the shelter, the material underneath you, and the people nearby.
